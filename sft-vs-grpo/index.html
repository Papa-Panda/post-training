Train SFT and GRPO on the same tasks —
where do the results actually differ?

Neither one dominates across the board. The stable conclusion, verified across multiple same-task, same-model comparisons, is a division of labor: SFT installs what the model doesn't yet know; GRPO sharpens what it can already do a little. Getting the order right matters far more than arguing about which is stronger. The new agent-behavior evidence below adds a second lens: accuracy is not behavior — length, turns, tool calls, abstention, and error recovery move in different, sometimes opposite, directions.

What SFT does

InstallFormat, knowledge, new reasoning patterns. Fastest, most stable, cheapest gains in-distribution; the cost is memorizing trajectories, a narrower distribution, OOD drop, and forgetting of old abilities.

What GRPO does

SharpenTurns Pass@k into Pass@1. Better OOD retention, stronger rule generalization; it requires that the model can already succeed occasionally, and a verifiable reward.

The production optimum

SFT → GRPOThe mainstream recipe behind DeepSeek-R1 and Tülu 3. Don't overtrain SFT, or entropy collapse will strangle the GRPO signal that follows.

01

Start with the mechanism: why the results must differ

The two methods fundamentally optimize different things. SFT is behavior cloning on fixed expert tokens; GRPO is policy gradient on trajectories the model samples itself, scored by outcome reward. This one fact determines every difference that follows.

SFT · SUPERVISED FINE-TUNING

Learn how the expert writes

Loss = − Σ log π(y*_t | x, y*_{<t})  ·  every token of every demonstration weighted equally
  • DataOffline, fixed expert / teacher trajectories (off-policy)
  • Can teachSequences the model would never sample on its own — new knowledge, new formats, hard problems with a zero pass rate
  • StrengthsStable, cheap (one forward + backward pass per token), largest in-distribution gains
  • WeaknessesMemorizes style and incidental choices; at inference it runs on its own distribution → exposure bias, and one wrong step compounds in multi-turn tasks; large updates, prone to forgetting
  • Weight fingerprintRajani et al.: update magnitudes far larger than GRPO's, with more movement in middle-layer MLPs — hypothesized as the source of OOD degradation (freezing experiments still inconclusive)
GRPO · GROUP RELATIVE POLICY OPTIMIZATION

Reinforce the right answers among its own attempts

A_i = (r_i − mean(r_group)) / std(r_group)  ·  sample G completions per prompt, weight by relative reward within the group, no critic needed
  • DataOnline rollouts generated by the model itself (on-policy) + verifiable reward
  • Can teachCan only reweight sequences it can already sample — turning occasional success into reliable success, and composing existing atomic skills
  • StrengthsScored by outcome rather than surface form, so it generalizes better across rule / visual variants; gentler updates, better retention of old OOD abilities
  • WeaknessesExpensive and unstable: G samples per prompt (DeepSeekMath used G=64); when a group is all-correct or all-wrong, the advantage is 0 and the gradient is 0 — nothing is learned
  • Zero-gradient conditionThis is the key to every GRPO failure: problems that are too easy (saturated) and too hard (acc=0) produce no signal — DAPO's dynamic sampling and ReLIFT's routing of hard problems to SFT both exist to work around it
The mechanism in one sentence: SFT can teach token sequences the model has never produced; GRPO can only choose among sequences it can already produce. The former expands the boundary, the latter raises purity — the later debate between Yue et al. and Yuan / Cheng is essentially this one fact playing out under different conditions.

The cleanest same-task, same-model comparison — Rajani et al., “Scalpel vs. Hammer”

Same set of math problems, same model, similar hyperparameters, 20 checkpoints each (ICML 2025 workshop; the authors call it preliminary). The conclusion isn't who wins, but that magnitude is proportional to cost: SFT gains more in-distribution and loses more OOD (knowledge tasks such as MMLU); GRPO gains modestly but basically preserves OOD. SFT's KL from the base model shoots up early; GRPO's grows gradually.

Agentic / Coding: RL's margin over SFT is surprisingly small

  • Multi-Harness study (Qwen3-8B, SWE-bench Verified): from the same starting point and the same data, RL beats SFT by only +0.48 ~ +0.77 pp, and the difference is not significant on unseen harnesses; meanwhile the harnesses themselves differ by 7.13 pp — choosing the harness matters more than choosing RL.
  • PivotRL (Qwen3-30B-A3B, four agentic domains): on identical trajectories vs SFT, +4.17 pp average in-domain and +10.04 pp OOD retention (SFT averages −9.83 pp); but on SWE-Bench Verified alone it actually loses to SFT (32.67% vs 37.40%, per secondary-abstract figures, coexisting with the abstract's averages). It also matches end-to-end RL with about 1/4 the rollout rounds and about 1/5.5 the wall-clock time.
  • An extreme forgetting case: in the PivotRL paper, after SFT on the terminal domain, AIME25 falls from 86.04% to 21.56% (−64.5 pp).

03

The core debate: does RL create new capability, or only amplify what exists?

This is the most fiercely debated question since 2025, and the one most easily misled by a single Pass@1 table. Both sides have hard evidence; what matters is the conditions under which each holds.

Evidence A · Amplification

Yue et al. (NeurIPS 2025 Oral): RLVR does not expand the boundary

Across multiple model families, 6 RLVR algorithms, and math/code/vision: RL models beat the base at small k (Pass@1) but lose at large k — the longer training runs, the narrower the set of solvable problems. All RLVR algorithms perform similarly and far below the base model's ceiling. Distillation (strong-teacher SFT), by contrast, genuinely expands the boundary. The original DeepSeekMath paper had in fact observed the same phenomenon early on: RL improves majority voting, but not “at least one correct in K tries.”

arXiv:2504.13837

Evidence B · Composition

Yuan / Cheng: when atomic skills are in place, RL synthesizes new combinations

In synthetic tasks, once the model has separately mastered the atomic skills f and g, RL learns the never-demonstrated composition h = g∘f, and generalizes to compositions of depth two and beyond, plus cross-task transfer; SFT on the same data learns no such composition at all (Cheng: composite SFT scores 90.3% in-domain but only 18.4% zero-shot). The precondition is stated plainly: without mastery of the atomic skills, RL is still only amplification.

Yuan et al. arXiv:2509.25123 (ICLR 2026) · Cheng et al. arXiv:2512.01970

Synthesis (the safest current formulation): RL does not invent a brand-new atomic skill out of nothing. What it can do is ① surface latent capability (1-shot RLVR: a single example takes MATH500 from 36.0% → 73.6% — elicitation, not teaching; beyond format fixes there is still +8.6 points of latent capability released); ② compose existing atomic skills into new solution methods. SFT / distillation injects the atomic skills and new knowledge. Conflating these three things is the root of most “RL is everything / RL is useless” arguments.

Pass@1 ↑

RL's certain payoff: the distribution concentrates on high-reward modes, so greedy / single-sample decoding is more accurate. If deployment samples once, this is a real gain.

Entropy ↓ · Pass@k (large k) flat or ↓

The other side of the same coin: diversity is cut. If deployment relies on self-consistency voting, tree search, or multiple samples, Pass@1 overstates RL's value — track Pass@k and entropy alongside it.

SFT narrows too

SFT also collapses onto demonstration style. In Rath et al., RFT actually widened — whether things narrow depends on data construction, not just the algorithm's name.

04

Order effects: why the mainstream is SFT → GRPO, not the reverse

Ding et al.'s Plasticity-Ceiling framework states this most completely: the final ceiling = SFT foundation + RL plasticity. Pure RL rises fast and caps fast (plateauing at ~71.5 points within ~25 exaFLOPs); the scale of the SFT data is the primary determinant of the ceiling.

Step 1 · Cold start
SFT

Install format and basic patterns first. Chu et al.: RL directly without SFT fails; and RL cannot rescue an extremely overfit SFT checkpoint either.

Step 2 · Handoff point
Light SFT

Hand off in the “stable / slightly overfit” zone. Pick trajectories by minimum validation loss; don't pick the checkpoint with the highest Pass@1 as the RL starting point.

Step 3 · Sharpening
GRPO

Convert Pass@k into Pass@1. Monitor within-group advantage variance and entropy; DAPO's dynamic sampling (dropping zero-variance groups) lifts AIME 2024 from naive GRPO's 30 to 50.

Step 4 · Recovery
RL → SFT

DeepSeek-R1 stage three: collect the good trajectories RL discovered, via rejection sampling, back into SFT, repairing readability and general-capability regressions. This is a different thing — don't confuse it with SFT→RL.

Better than a sequential pipeline: interleaved ReLIFT

Ma et al. (ICLR 2026): RL is good at problems “within capability,” SFT at the hardest “beyond capability” ones (acc=0, where RL gets no signal at all). ReLIFT is RL-led, interleaving SFT only on the hardest problems, with solutions collected online: 52.6% average across 5 math + 1 OOD benchmarks, 6.7 points above prior RLVR, using only ~13% of the demonstration data (SFT-only baseline 49.0%). Random interleaving gets only 45.5% — what you interleave matters more than interleaving itself.

arXiv:2506.07527

Divide by difficulty: DeReason

Assigning low-reasoning-intensity (knowledge-type) data to SFT and hard problems to RL beats random splits, pure SFT, and pure RL; and with medium-quality responses, pure RLVR from the base “consistently and significantly” loses to SFT. Joint / synchronous SFT+RL (LUFFY, SRFT, etc.) underperforms the sequential approach in Ding et al.'s comparisons and is less stable.

arXiv:2603.11193 (author details not verified in the sources read)

07

Negative results, pitfalls, and unanswered questions

The better the SFT, the worse GRPO can get — rank reversal

Aphale & Liu (Qwen2.5-Coder-3B, 3-seed mean): as SFT depth increases, pre-RL Pass@1 rises, but GRPO's peak Pass@10 falls monotonically 0.806 → 0.481. Mechanism: entropy collapse → within-group advantage variance p(1−p) → 0. Practical rule: don't hand GRPO the SFT checkpoint with the highest Pass@1 — watch entropy and Pass@64. On DeepSeek-Coder-6.7B the ranking is only compressed, not reversed — strong model dependence.

An “RL beats SFT” claim reporting only in-distribution Pass@1 is basically incomplete

Rajani: SFT's in-distribution gains are larger to begin with; the cost shows up OOD. Chu et al. (ICML 2025): SFT can win or tie in-domain, then collapse when the rules change — e.g., continued SFT on V-IRL-L drops 80.8% → 1.3%, while RL climbs gradually 35.7% → 45.0% on an unseen action space, where SFT gets only 2.5%. Evaluation must report OOD and large-k Pass@k together.

Evaluation itself is fragile

Pass@k is extremely sensitive to prompt format, temperature, and the grader: one 2026 preprint (not peer-reviewed, preliminary) shows that after giving each arm its own best prompt, a +36.5 pp MATH-500 Pass@1 advantage shrinks to +12.27 pp. Whenever you cite a comparison number, attach the prompt, temperature, k, and base-model control.

The base model decides everything — don't transplant conclusions across families

Qwen models, with heavy math pretraining, show far larger “RL gains” than the Llama family; 1-shot RLVR works precisely because the capability is already in pretraining. Switching base-model families requires re-testing.

Still-open questions

  • A clean same-prompt, same-FLOPs cost table does not exist — no primary paper compares SFT vs GRPO on identical hardware, so cost figures remain qualitative for now.
  • Whether long-horizon RL (ProRL etc.) can truly break Yue et al.'s Pass@k ceiling — claimed (arXiv:2505.24864), but not independently verified in this round of research.
  • PivotRL's single-domain SWE figures — the abstract average (+4.17 pp) and the secondary abstract's per-domain numbers (32.67% vs SFT 37.40%) coexist without full reconciliation; cite the abstract average as the primary figure and the per-domain number as a caveat.
  • Rajani's MLP hypothesis — “SFT moves middle-layer MLPs more, causing OOD degradation” remains a hypothesis; freezing experiments are inconsistent.
How Jun should read this: if you read only three papers — start with Chu et al. (2501.17161) to build the “memorization vs generalization” intuition, then Yue et al. (2504.13837) to calibrate expectations about RL's boundary, and finally Ding et al. (2512.11470) for actionable SFT→RL handoff rules. Look up the rest in the table as needed.
08

Agent behavior after training: what actually changes — and what doesn't line up

This section integrates the 2026-11-19 behavior research. Headline, stated plainly: no single paper compares SFT vs GRPO on the same agentic tasks across the full behavior battery — length, turns, tool calls, retry, diversity, and stopping. The evidence is fragmented, each paper measures a slice, and the direction flips by domain and reward. That gap is itself the finding.

Read this before quoting any behavior claim: SFT copies the demonstrated form; RL re-optimises form for outcome. That is why RL can lengthen reasoning in maths (DeepSeek-R1, WebExplorer, ASearcher) and shorten / collapse it in tool use (AutoTool, ASTER interaction collapse). Any blanket claim like “GRPO makes agents more concise” or “GRPO makes them longer” is contradicted inside this literature.

Why the direction flips — mechanisms (verified / well-supported)

  • SFT = behavior cloning. Cross-entropy pushes up every demonstrated token with no constraint on policy drift (PSFT framing: “policy gradient with strictly positive advantages,” advantage always 1). It reproduces surface form — format, phrasing, tool-call syntax, trajectory shape — and teaches the format RL needs to get any reward on a non-instruction-following base.
  • RL = outcome optimisation. Reward is usually only for the final answer / verifier pass, regardless of form. Any trajectory shape that reaches reward is reinforced; shapes that do not are suppressed. Hence both discovery (R1 self-verification, ToolRL proactiveness, ASearcher >100-turn search) and collapse to the cheapest rewarded shape (AutoTool short reasoning, ASTER shallow verification, Mercor DPPO short turns).
  • Entropy is the mediator. Both methods can collapse entropy — SFT by sharpening on a narrow teacher distribution, RL by reinforcing currently-best trajectories. Low entropy → loss of exploration → premature convergence to short / shallow policies. Entropy constraints, restoration, and trust regions are all attempts to manage this one variable.
  • Covariate shift. SFT trains only on expert-induced states; at deployment the model's own errors put it in states the teacher never visited. RL trains on its own rollouts, so it visits — and learns to recover from — its own error states.
  • Reward hacking as a class. The report cites the Reward Hacking survey (arXiv:2604.13602) and related work: policies first exploit feature-level correlates (verbosity, sycophancy), then representation- and evaluator-level shortcuts. Length penalties can themselves create a shortcut — extreme brevity / omitted steps.

Hypotheses, labelled as such in the report: that KL-regularised RL is “implicitly regularised” and therefore generalises (a theoretical framing, not a measured behavior in Chu); Mercor's “deliberate multi-step work” reading of turn/token metrics (authors disclaim rigorous justification); and that ToolRL's emergent proactiveness is “genuine reasoning, not memorised patterns” (no ablation in the reviewed material distinguishes the two).

Negative / null behavior findings

  • PivotRL loses to SFT on SWE-Bench Verified (32.67 vs 37.40) despite winning on average (+4.17 pp in-domain, +10.04 pp OOD retention). Domain matters; averages hide it.
  • Mercor ablations are nulls for behavior-shaping knobs: overlong filtering −1.5 pts; adaptive length penalty neutral-to-negative at every setting; DPPO vs GLM-5 loss within noise on score (behavior differs, score does not).
  • Cold-start SFT can hurt downstream RL. ASTER: sparse / short-horizon SFT priors “fail to outperform Zero.” ToolRL: SFT-initialised GRPO underperforms cold-start GRPO; ToolRL reports GRPO cold-start beats same-data SFT by ~15% and ~+17% over base (see paper card).
  • RL without SFT fails entirely on Llama-3.2-Vision-11B in Chu §5.4 — a null for “RL alone changes behavior” when the base cannot follow instructions.
  • Mercor HLE / GPQA: no regression, no improvement after agent RL — change is task-bound, not a general capability shift.
  • Shorter is not automatically better: in the ProCredit ablation cited in the report, discounting turn-level returns shortens trajectories and loses hard tasks; adding final progress alone does not improve performance.
  • No paper in the set reports “RL changed accuracy with zero observable behavior change” as a headline. The closest are Mercor's DPPO-vs-GLM-5 (score tie, behavior differs — the inverse) and the HLE/GPQA null (accuracy tie, behavior unmeasured).

Open questions — marked plainly

  • No direct SFT-vs-GRPO behavior battery on the same agent tasks was found. Chu uses PPO, not GRPO; ToolRL / PivotRL control for data but do not publish turn/length/diversity tables; Mercor has no SFT arm; DaisyRose-27B (HF blog, 2026-11-19) has no SFT arm and no behavior metrics — do not cite it as evidence for SFT-vs-RL behavior change. Its reported figures are Terminal-Bench 4.1 pass@1 5.6% → 12.0%, 1,200 curated tasks, sessions up to 1M tokens via harness summarisation, with >100 tool calls / >256k output tokens per attempt as task allowances, not a measured before/after change.
  • “DPPO makes behavior more concise” is verified only as more, shorter turns; implied total assistant tokens rise slightly. Do not report it as net conciseness.
  • LoopTool (arXiv:2511.09148) — no behavior table was found in the reviewed material; its contribution is the data loop (BFCL-v3 74.93% vs 66.34% base; ACEBench 73.4% vs 67.1%), not a measured behavior change. Do not cite it as a behavior-change measurement.
  • Whether SFT and RL converge to similar behavior on any agent task: no paper in the set tests this directly. ASTER's budget-alignment result (training with a 50-call budget: 83.0% at 256 inference calls vs 77.7% for 10-call training; at only 2 inference calls, 10-call training wins 51.5% vs 32.3%) suggests behavior is contingent on training / inference budgets, not convergent.
  • Diversity is almost always proxied by entropy / Pass@K, not by trajectory clustering or action-distribution distance; stopping behavior is rarely measured except via overlong / context-exhaustion rates; most comparisons confound data (teacher trajectories vs own rollouts) with objective (cross-entropy vs reward) — PivotRL and ToolRL's same-data SFT baselines are the exceptions that control for data.

Take away on one page

SFT

Can't → can. Installs format, knowledge, new patterns; cheap and stable; beware memorization, forgetting, and overtraining.

GRPO

Occasionally can → reliably can. Turns Pass@k into Pass@1; needs a verifiable reward and a nonzero success rate; beware entropy collapse.

SFT → GRPO

The default production recipe. When the hardest problems sit at acc=0, interleave SFT back in (ReLIFT) instead of forcing RL.
